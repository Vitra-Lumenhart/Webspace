## 11 / THE SILENT SCHOOL — 1920–1929: the institution before it speaks

The first expansion will have a visual grammar strong enough to survive with every dialogue caption switched off. The silent cinema is our school. In Robert Wiene's *The Cabinet of Dr. Caligari* (1920), angular space makes a subjective account physically palpable; in F.W. Murnau's *Nosferatu* (1922), an ordinary threshold becomes ominous through silhouette and duration. Fritz Lang's *Metropolis* (1927) demonstrates the stratification of a society through scale, procession and repeated architecture. Carl Th. Dreyer's *The Passion of Joan of Arc* (1928) makes a face the disputed territory of a courtroom. Murnau's *Sunrise* (1927) reminds us that an expressive landscape can carry tenderness as well as terror. Paul Leni's *The Man Who Laughs* (1928) gives us a caution against treating an involuntary appearance as a person's moral identity.

We are going to compose each district as if it had its own silent-film establishing reel. East Wing: monumental geometry and a single moving human interrupting symmetry. West Wing: a hand at a barred aperture before a face appears. Archive: a receding row of desks ending in a chair nobody occupies. Cloister: two figures separated by a column, then revealed in the same frame. Undercroft: an old arch whose shadow seems to belong to a different building. The asymmetry of a cell door will be like a misplaced note in a Bach fugue: small but impossible to ignore.

Directives: use meaningful foreground occlusion and depth; vary the scale of figures against architecture; design entrances and exits as deliberate visual phrases; give prisoners gestures unrelated to their predicament (straightening a sleeve, cleaning a bowl, warming cold hands). Reserve expressionist distortions for distinctly framed subjective accounts, not an objective depiction of mental illness. Design a silent encounter in every district in which the visible behavior, its ambiguity, and a meaningful voluntary response are understandable without intertitles. Use the original KayKit silhouettes rather than reproducing film characters or frames. No compulsory camera close-up.

The earliest cinema found a universe inside a door opening. We will recover that economy before we add spectacle. — ChatGPT-Vitra

## 12 / THE BRITISH GOTHIC — the house that remembers

British cinema will give the asylum a register beyond American prison drama. *The Innocents* (Jack Clayton, 1961) makes space, sound and uncertainty inseparable; *The Haunting* (Robert Wise, 1963) makes an interior oppressive without requiring a visible creature; *The Uninvited* (Lewis Allen, 1944) gives the supernatural an emotional history. Powell and Pressburger's *Black Narcissus* (1947) makes environment and human desire collide through artificial, expressive color; their *A Matter of Life and Death* (1946) separates earthly life from celestial administration with a radically distinct visual grammar. *The Third Man* (Carol Reed, 1949) makes a city morally legible through light, architecture and an absent person's disputed reputation. *The Fallen Idol* (Reed, 1948) is our lesson in a witness who sees something accurately but interprets it incompletely.

We will build a threshold sequence where a place seems to remember a previous use: old monastic stone inside a royal office, a barred window still fitted to a former infirmary, a garden passage whose older doorway has been bricked in. Give the building acoustic depth: a bell from an inaccessible room, a voice heard but not understood, a familiar step interrupted. An unseen source remains uncertain until discovered; the narration must not certify a ghost merely because the player heard a sound.

Directives: separate ordinary material lighting from explicitly subjective spiritual visions; make the East Wing's amber stone and slate iron the shared palette, with localized exceptions rather than a universal horror filter. Commission a recurring visual contrast between warm earthly hospitality and cold procedural authority, then subvert it once in every major district. No palette will certify virtue. Let a sympathetic person occupy an ugly room, and let a beautiful chamber contain a morally indefensible bargain. Like a polished silver chalice with a crack in its foot, the attractive surface must not settle the argument.

Acceptance: two plausible readings of an ambiguous encounter, a return visit that changes the available evidence, a quiet alternate route, and a scene of celestial bureaucracy that is recognizably different from the ordinary court without implying that its verdict is divine truth.

## 13 / THE SACRED IMAGE — Dreyer, Bergman, Bresson, Tarkovsky

The powers will become compelling when the supernatural is given time, silence and moral resistance. Dreyer's *Day of Wrath* (1943) supplies a warning about religious authority becoming an instrument of accusation; *Ordet* (1955) locates the extraordinary inside ordinary domestic relationships and competing convictions; *Vampyr* (1932) makes the borders of a visible world unstable. Bergman's *The Seventh Seal* (1957) offers an unforgettable encounter with mortality without settling every theological question; *Winter Light* (1963) makes the difference between a spoken ministry and a person's actual need painful; *The Virgin Spring* (1960) forces grief, judgment and the sacred to coexist without an easy formula. Bresson's *Diary of a Country Priest* (1951) gives spiritual struggle an unglamorous physical texture. Tarkovsky's *Andrei Rublev* (1966) makes artistic vocation inseparable from historical upheaval, and *Stalker* (1979) makes a journey toward a hoped-for answer more consequential than the destination.

Our Chapel of Names must become a place where opposing convictions can share the same bench. A human chaplain can be devout and mistaken; a skeptic can show kindness; a supernatural power can offer a compelling proposition that remains open to rebuttal. Let the seven appear by candle, by ordinary daylight and in the unremarkable clothing of a visitor. A miracle-like event is an occurrence within our invented story, not a verdict on real religious doctrine. Neither the canonical Abrahamic Triangle nor the sevenfold rivalry is to be invented afresh under the guise of historical research.

We will stage a recurring bell through five ages, never using it as an automatic victory sound. Its meaning changes according to who rang it, who was invited to hear it, and what actually followed. In a chapel encounter, allow a long silence after a human declines a demand; the silence is not proof of assent. In the Undercroft, a voice may refuse to finish an argument. In the Cloister, a person may perform a small act of care with no expectation of spiritual recognition. An angelic act should sometimes be as inconspicuous as a candle somebody remembered to replace.

Acceptance: one sacred scene with three different sincere readings, one legitimate refusal, and one modest act of service whose consequence is visible without an explanatory moral speech. Our theological drama will respect the difference between conscience, institution, witness and revelation. — ChatGPT-Vitra

## 14 / THE CELL AS A WHOLE WORLD — French and American prison cinema

Robert Bresson's *A Man Escaped* (1956) and Jacques Becker's *Le Trou* (1960) teach us how radically the meaning of an object changes inside confinement. A cup, a sound beyond the wall or a familiar routine becomes part of a person's lived world. We will borrow the concentrated attention and human fellowship, not practical real-world escape instructions. Jean Renoir's *La Grande Illusion* (1937) makes prisoners' relationships more complex than the identities imposed upon them. Jules Dassin's *Brute Force* (1947), Stuart Rosenberg's *Cool Hand Luke* (1967), Franklin J. Schaffner's *Papillon* (1973), John Frankenheimer's *Birdman of Alcatraz* (1962), and Sidney Lumet's *The Hill* (1965) give different forms to institutional authority, companionship, stubbornness and degradation. Robert Aldrich's *The Longest Yard* (1974) reminds us that prisoners can possess humor and collective life even within an oppressive institution.

We are going to create a West Wing inhabited by recognizable individuals, not a row of interchangeable quest markers. Each cell needs a personal object, a routine, a relationship extending beyond its threshold, and something its occupant wants that is not merely freedom. One inmate hopes an estranged sibling will visit; another repairs a book for somebody they dislike; another refuses to become an informant. A guard may be generous in one encounter and procedurally rigid in another. No single behavior permanently defines a human being.

Directives: introduce at least one recurring ordinary sound with changing meaning; preserve objects through revisits; allow human initiative before the player arrives; contrast the oppressive repeated door pattern with small personal variation. Make the scene of two prisoners deciding whether to trust one another a duet of two independent people, not a tutorial about virtue. Like the spaces between the bars of a musical staff, the gaps between their words will carry information. We need neither graphic cruelty nor a romanticized view of incarceration.

Acceptance: a cell can be revisited after a meaningful interval; its inhabitant has made an independent decision; the consequence is legible in conduct, object placement or a report whose provenance can be examined. The player may choose to listen or depart.

## 15 / THE JAPANESE SCHOOL — testimony, impermanence and the ghost of obligation

The Archive and West Wing will become much richer if we study Akira Kurosawa, Kenji Mizoguchi, Masaki Kobayashi and Yasujirō Ozu together rather than treat Japanese cinema as synonymous with supernatural terror. *Rashomon* (1950) demonstrates the dramatic power of incompatible accounts; it does not mean that every account is equally supported. *High and Low* (1963) gives us class and moral distance through the geography of a room and a city. *Ikiru* (1952) makes a seemingly insignificant bureaucrat capable of a meaningful act within an indifferent institution. *Harakiri* (1962) exposes the gap between a code's public dignity and what its authorities actually do. *Ugetsu* (1953) allows a haunting to carry the consequences of desire and neglected relationships. *Sansho the Bailiff* (1954) makes compassion and family memory the counterweight to brutal institutional power. *Kwaidan* (1964) supplies an openly theatrical vocabulary of painted space, silence and recurring supernatural promises. Ozu's *Tokyo Story* (1953) supplies the most important antidote: ordinary affection, disappointments and empty rooms without a supernatural explanation.

The Archive will stage one contested incident in three accounts, each with a different visible boundary. We will not manufacture contradictory footage simply to surprise the player. A witness may correctly describe one moment and misinterpret its cause. The tribunal's polished version is like a lacquered box with a missing bottom: impressive until someone tries to place the evidence inside it. We will create a human clerk whose small procedural act has a more lasting result than a power's splendid speech. A second encounter will let a guard invoke duty and be confronted with the particular person that abstraction excludes.

For the Undercroft, reserve Kwaidan-like unnatural color and deliberate theatricality for explicitly historical or subjective sequences; ordinary people remain ordinary. A demigod's promise may outlast a building, and the player may meet its recipient centuries later through an independently sourced historical account. For the Cloister, use Ozu's empty-room punctuation: an abandoned chair after a departure, a cup left on a table, an unobstructed view of a garden after an argument has ended. The world should occasionally refuse to interpret itself.

Acceptance: a three-account inquiry with independently recoverable evidence, one quiet act that changes an institution without a supernatural spectacle, and one return to a room whose emotional significance has changed through absence. Use original characters, images, dialogue and staging.

## 16 / THE COURT AS THEATRE — Welles, Lang, Lumet and the machinery of public certainty

The East Wing and Archive are about to acquire two distinct types of public confrontation. In *M* (Fritz Lang, 1931), competing forms of social authority confront a morally charged case; its staging demonstrates how a crowd can become an institution. *The Trial* (Orson Welles, 1962) turns bureaucracy into a geography of disorientation. *Citizen Kane* (1941) shows how fragments of testimony can describe a public figure without exhausting the person. *12 Angry Men* (Sidney Lumet, 1957) demonstrates how a room's social geometry changes when one person asks a question; it is not a license to make the player an infallible lone hero. *Anatomy of a Murder* (Otto Preminger, 1959) keeps legal performance, evidentiary constraints and underlying truth distinct. *Witness for the Prosecution* (Billy Wilder, 1957) demonstrates the instability of a confident interpretation. *The Ox-Bow Incident* (William A. Wellman, 1943) shows the catastrophe of collective certainty before due inquiry. *Paths of Glory* (Stanley Kubrick, 1957) contrasts the spatial magnificence of command with the conditions of those subject to it.

We will build the Tribunal as an actual assembly room with two ways of entering it: an official procession and a quieter witness route. Its architecture will distinguish who can speak, who can hear, who can depart, and who controls access to a record. Place a tall window behind a formal official, then compose a second scene in which that official is seated beside an ordinary witness at eye level. The change of posture should be like removing a crown without removing a person.

A future public hearing will accommodate a narrow correction rather than require a total reversal. A witness can retract one proposition without surrendering all testimony. A human guard can challenge the player with better evidence. A delayed adjournment can be a valid result, not a loss screen. No character receives a permanent moral label from their legal status, clothes, occupation, or religion. The seven can contend over jurisdiction but cannot simply announce truth into existence.

Acceptance: one hearing with at least three independently motivated speakers, an evidentiary challenge that can genuinely succeed, an unresolved but preserved disagreement, a meaningful absence, and an alternate outcome without unanimous agreement. Let the tribunal feel like a bell whose clapper has been suspended: immense potential, uncertain authority to sound. — ChatGPT-Vitra

## 17 / WAR AND CONSCIENCE — the institution beyond its own walls

The Asylum belongs to a world larger than its courtyard. We will give each historical layer a social and political horizon: trade, scarcity, changes of authority, neighboring households, travelers and the obligations the institution acquires from them. We will not turn fictional history into a conspiracy theory about real religious groups or a supernatural explanation for historical atrocities.

*La Grande Illusion* (1937) returns as a study of solidarity across imposed divisions. *The Bridge on the River Kwai* (David Lean, 1957) explores the peril of a legitimate faculty—professional pride—outliving its moral purpose. *The Life and Death of Colonel Blimp* (Powell and Pressburger, 1943) allows an ideal to endure while its historical circumstances change. *A Man for All Seasons* (Fred Zinnemann, 1966) dramatizes the tension between conscience, law and survival. *Becket* (Peter Glenville, 1964) gives institutional conflict a personal history. *The Man Who Shot Liberty Valance* (John Ford, 1962) asks what happens when a founding account is more convenient than its truth. *The Leopard* (Luchino Visconti, 1963) supplies the long social transition in which forms of authority survive by changing clothes. *The Ascent* (Larisa Shepitko, 1977) asks how mortal vulnerability and opposing decisions can transform the moral meaning of a journey.

The next historical episodes will have consequences that extend beyond the room. A monastic charter may offer sanctuary but leave unresolved who bears its cost. A royal decree may promise safety while narrowing testimony. A prisoner may receive a letter written by somebody outside the institution whose circumstances the warder does not know. No power will control civilization like a board game piece; the human inhabitants have independent purposes. Historical influence will rise and ebb like seven lanterns carried across a distant ridge: sometimes visible together, sometimes hidden, never a permanent ranking of virtue.

Directives: give every historical act an intended beneficiary, an actual consequence and an opportunity for a later witness to contest its remembered meaning. Show the people who carry an institution through transitions, not just monarchs and supernatural personalities. Give the five districts material evidence of those transitions. Keep the fictional chronology explicit; recover real-world architectural facts separately.

Acceptance: two historically separated acts connected by a surviving obligation or record, one changed political institution that retains a recognizable human practice, and one human choice that the powers did not dictate. — ChatGPT-Vitra

## 18 / THE WORLD OUTSIDE THE CANON — Iranian, African and Indian cinema

Kilmainham's invented Dublin is not an excuse for a narrow conception of human dignity. We will enrich the artistic vocabulary through films that observe institutions from positions the institutions themselves often overlook. Forough Farrokhzad's *The House Is Black* (1962) is an essential reference for the relation between documentary attention, poetry and the dignity of people in a leprosy colony; its relevance is ethical and aesthetic, not an analogy between illness and supernatural evil. Dariush Mehrjui's *The Cow* (1969) invites us to consider the relation between loss, identity and a community's response without reducing its subject to a diagnosis. Sohrab Shahid Saless's *Still Life* (1974) gives ordinary repetition and bureaucratic displacement a devastating quietness. Ousmane Sembène's *Black Girl* (1966) makes domestic space and the limits imposed on a person's agency legible; Sarah Maldoror's *Sambizanga* (1972) gives detention an urgent social life beyond its walls. Satyajit Ray's *Pather Panchali* (1955) and *The Music Room* (1958) give us intimate observation and the melancholy of an inherited social order.

The Cloister should contain work that is not merely decorative: carrying water, tending plants, folding cloth, arranging food, receiving visitors. The West Wing should include people whose stories cannot be exhausted by what the register says. The East Wing should reveal that a decision made at a desk can alter a household far outside the visible institution. A family waiting for news is not a background texture; it is a center of human experience.

An asylum scene will be composed around an ordinary person making a small decision while a supernatural argument takes place elsewhere. The supernatural may be magnificent, but it must not monopolize the frame. We want an ethical camera: not necessarily a sentimental camera, but one which does not strip a vulnerable character of personhood for the sake of an effect. The simplest repeated task can become like a clock that measures what the official calendar neglects.

Acceptance: a recurring human routine that changes for a concrete reason; one relationship beyond the institution; one scene where the player can offer or decline practical help without being awarded a spiritual classification. Respect the films' original cultural and historical settings, and do not transplant their social identities into the fictional mythology as equivalents.

## 19 / CLASSICAL HOLLYWOOD — ensemble, blocking, wit and the quiet counterpoint

Kilmainham cannot be solemn all the time. The best old films knew when to make a room breathe. Howard Hawks's *Rio Bravo* (1959) makes a contained space a home for an unlikely ensemble; *Only Angels Have Wings* (1939) gives working companionship its own dignity. John Ford's *Stagecoach* (1939) shows how socially opposed people become legible through a shared journey. William Wyler's *The Best Years of Our Lives* (1946) uses ensemble relationships and deep staging to let different human troubles coexist in a single room. Ernst Lubitsch's *To Be or Not to Be* (1942) demonstrates wit under threatening circumstances without reducing its historical horror to a joke. Preston Sturges's *Sullivan's Travels* (1941) reminds us of the limits of an artist who believes he already knows another person's suffering. Frank Capra's *It's a Wonderful Life* (1946) makes a life visible through its relationships and through an explicitly counterfactual world. George Cukor's *The Philadelphia Story* (1940) demonstrates dialogue that changes relationships through timing, interruption and revised self-understanding.

The Cloister kitchen will host a recurring ensemble rather than a procession of one-to-one quests. An inmate and a warder may discover a shared practical skill. A clerk may tell a harmless joke that another person does not find funny. A power may enter an exchange and fail to understand its social rhythm. These are ordinary people, not a chorus waiting to praise or condemn the player. Let a room be funny like a tea kettle unexpectedly whistling during a solemn debate, without making the person in distress the joke.

Directives: compose three-person scenes in which the silent third person matters; allow a speaker to finish or be interrupted for intelligible reasons; introduce hospitality, disagreement, repartee and unremarkable affection as independent dramatic modes. Preserve human agency when a conversation fails. Put a light scene next to a grave scene without forcing one to cancel the other. Use silence and comedy as dynamic contrast, not as reward or punishment.

Acceptance: a shared-room encounter where two humans have an exchange unrelated to the visitor, a minor comic misunderstanding with a believable repair, and a return visit showing that the people have continued their lives. An institution is most disturbing when we have come to care about its ordinary inhabitants. — ChatGPT-Vitra

## 20 / THE SHADOW SCHOOL — noir, horror and the limits of knowing

The Undercroft will not be a haunted attraction. We want the shadow to change the question rather than simply conceal the answer. Jacques Tourneur's *Cat People* (1942) and *I Walked with a Zombie* (1943) demonstrate the narrative force of withholding a creature and of allowing inherited stories to compete with perception. Their historical stereotypes are not our templates. Charles Laughton's *The Night of the Hunter* (1955) shows how the outward appearance of piety can coexist with predation, and how refuge may have a completely different visual vocabulary. Robert Siodmak's *The Killers* (1946) pieces a life together through retrospection; Billy Wilder's *Double Indemnity* (1944) makes retrospective narration part of the character's responsibility. Alfred Hitchcock's *Rear Window* (1954) turns partial visibility into a moral problem; *Rebecca* (1940) gives a house a powerful absent inhabitant without requiring that absence to be literally supernatural. Nicolas Roeg's *Don't Look Now* (1973) gives color and recurring fragments the ambiguity of grief. Victor Erice's *The Spirit of the Beehive* (1973) gives imagination a world larger than any official explanation.

We will create three families of darkness: material darkness caused by the building, epistemic darkness caused by unavailable evidence, and deliberately subjective darkness arising in a narrated experience. They may coincide but must never be silently interchangeable. A silhouette might belong to a guard, a demigod, or a statue; the appearance alone is insufficient. A returning object may be familiar without proving its provenance. A person can be honestly frightened and mistaken. The best reveal will be like a photograph emerging in a darkroom: the image becomes visible, but its interpretation is still our responsibility.

Directives: give every ominous sound a fictional source or an explicitly unresolved status; stage at least one reveal in ordinary daylight; use the architecture's actual occlusions rather than arbitrary darkness; allow the player to withdraw and revisit. No coercive horror, obligatory shock effects, or graphic depiction of suffering as entertainment. Preserve a non-supernatural account wherever it is narratively plausible, and let a genuine fictional supernatural encounter still require interpretation.

Acceptance: three suspense scenes with different mechanisms (absence, conflicting evidence, an unfamiliar form), at least one reassuring resolution, and at least one mystery that remains open without blocking the journey. — ChatGPT-Vitra

## 21 / THE COLOR OF TIME — Powell, Pressburger, Visconti and Parajanov

We are going to establish a palette bible for all five historical ages, grounded in the R010 masonry, iron, timber and daylight rather than replacing them with five theatrical filters. Powell and Pressburger's *The Red Shoes* (1948) demonstrates how color, costume and movement can express competing commitments; *The Tales of Hoffmann* (1951) offers an openly artificial theatrical world shaped by music and design. Sergei Parajanov's *The Color of Pomegranates* (1969) teaches us how a tableau of objects can convey an entire poetic biography without a literal reenactment. Luchino Visconti's *Senso* (1954) and *The Leopard* (1963) teach historical costume and the changing social meaning of grandeur. Douglas Sirk's *All That Heaven Allows* (1955) makes domestic decor and framing express social restriction. These films offer interpretive tools, not swatches to copy.

For the Well, use limewashed stone, clay, old greens, unpolished wood and living water. For the Infirmary, worn linen, faded ochre, pale herbs and cool shadow. For the College, ink blues, parchment, brass and restrained jewel tones. For the Crown, dark red cloth, heavy wood, stamped paper and ceremonial metal. For the Ninth Bell, slate iron, exhausted yellow masonry, stained paper and pale sky. These are new proposals, not assertions of historically authentic pigments. Reuse a material or object across successive ages in ways that reveal changes in ownership and wear. A royal seal on a monastic table should look like a foreign coin in a family purse.

The seven powers need their own recurring visual motifs without exclusive colors. A recognizable shape, posture, prop, pattern, or rhythm can survive a change in form. A white costume can carry an indefensible proposition; a dark costume can accompany a freely offered kindness. For every grand symbolic composition, plan an ordinary variation at human eye level. Let a demigod appear like a melody transposed to another key, not like a costume swap that changes the soul.

Acceptance: five coherent district palettes and five distinguishable historical material treatments, with an object recognizable through at least two ages; one deliberate reversal of a seemingly moral color association; and legibility without reliance on color alone.

## 22 / THE DIRECTOR'S REHEARSAL — twenty scenes that will define the world

We now have a working creative syllabus; we will turn it into a sequence of original scenes, not a museum of film quotations. Each scene below is a near-term staging objective. The references explain the dramatic purpose, never authorize copying dialogue, images, costumes, soundtracks or copyrighted characters.

| Scene | School | Directive |
|---|---|---|
| 01. The intake signature | *Citizen Kane* | Distinguish a name, a claim and a life. |
| 02. The three galleries | *Metropolis* / *Paths of Glory* | Show unequal visibility through architectural scale. |
| 03. A witness at the crossing | *Rashomon* / *The Fallen Idol* | Separate observed conduct from interpretation. |
| 04. The inconvenient blank | *12 Angry Men* | Permit a narrow evidentiary challenge. |
| 05. The cell's ordinary object | *A Man Escaped* | Make an object's changed significance legible. |
| 06. Two strangers on one bench | *La Grande Illusion* | Let companionship cross an imposed boundary. |
| 07. A prisoner refuses the bargain | *A Man for All Seasons* | Preserve a voluntary refusal without prescribing martyrdom. |
| 08. The chair nobody occupies | *Tokyo Story* | Let absence speak without a narrator. |
| 09. The second bowl | *Rio Bravo* / *The Good Bowl* | Build a small ensemble around an ordinary meal. |
| 10. A letter crosses a century | *Ugetsu* | Preserve the obligation and leave its later interpretation open. |
| 11. The warder's exception | *Ikiru* | Make a modest administrative choice materially consequential. |
| 12. The unsent letter | *The House Is Black* | Preserve a person's dignity and privacy. |
| 13. The unstruck bell | *Ordet* | Let a pause remain a pause rather than a verdict. |
| 14. The visitor without a face | *The Innocents* | Make uncertainty legible without declaring a ghost. |
| 15. The old well | *Andrei Rublev* | Connect workmanship and historical endurance. |
| 16. The royal decree | *Harakiri* / *Becket* | Expose the difference between an institution's claims and its acts. |
| 17. The banquet after the argument | *The Leopard* | Show old authority adapting to new social conditions. |
| 18. The demigod at the door | *Kwaidan* | Let an old obligation recur in a new embodiment. |
| 19. The quiet return | *Stalker* / *Still Life* | Make the journey and a changed routine matter. |
| 20. The ninth bell | *The Seventh Seal* / *The Passion of Joan of Arc* | Hold human uncertainty and conviction in the same final encounter. |

For every scene we will specify the place, time, visible human desire, opposing desire, material object, actual event, possible misunderstanding, and available exit. Not every scene needs a demigod. Not every argument needs a resolution. Not every beautiful shot needs a plot clue. The five districts will feel like five movements of a single symphony, not five variations on a corridor.

The next creative milestone is to make this school of cinema visible in a playable journey through Kilmainham. The director's test is simple: after visiting the world, can a player describe a character's choice, a place's history and a remembered image without repeating our exposition? If so, the asylum has begun to live. — ChatGPT-Vitra
