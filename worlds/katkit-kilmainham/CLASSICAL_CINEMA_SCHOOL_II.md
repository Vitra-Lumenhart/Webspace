# Kilmainham Asylum — Classical Cinema School, Book II

26 November 2026 · Original forward artistic direction. Companion to [Book I](CLASSICAL_CINEMA_SCHOOL.md). Six discussion notes preserved from [PR #24](https://github.com/Vitra-Lumenhart/Webspace/pull/24). Proposed scenes and artistic objectives, not shipped features.

## 23 / THE CHOREOGRAPHY OF AUTHORITY — bodies before words

The East Wing is going to become a theatre of distance. In *Alexander Nevsky* (Sergei Eisenstein, 1938), the massed silhouettes make opposing collective identities immediately legible; in *Ivan the Terrible, Part I* (1944), ritualized gestures and the architecture of power make a ruler's isolation visible. In *The Magnificent Ambersons* (Orson Welles, 1942), the staircase becomes a measure of family and social decline. *The Searchers* (John Ford, 1956) makes the doorway a frame for belonging and exclusion. *The Rules of the Game* (Jean Renoir, 1939) allows several people to pursue conflicting purposes in one continuous social space.

We will stage a change of authority without a single speech: a clerk waits at a table; a warder moves aside; an inmate remains seated; a visiting power discovers that nobody has made room for it. The grand entrance will be like a procession of chess pieces interrupted by a bird landing on the board. Its geometry tells us what the institution expects; the human gesture tells us that the expectation is not law.

Directives:
- Reserve axial symmetry for ceremonies, proclamations and formal examinations; break it with a person who is genuinely doing something else.
- Design five distinctive doorways, one per district, each with an immediately readable social relationship to the room beyond.
- Make stairs, railings and landings places where relationships are visible: waiting above, waiting below, walking together, choosing not to follow.
- Rehearse the same exchange at standing height, seated height and from a distant gallery. None is the definitive moral viewpoint.
- Give every major figure an ordinary recurring gesture that can change its meaning. A guard's habitual adjustment of a cuff may express composure in one scene and hesitation in another.

Acceptance: the transfer of attention and authority is legible in a silent original scene, while its ethical interpretation remains open. No borrowed film frames, choreography, or costumes. — ChatGPT-Vitra

## 24 / THE MUSICAL SCHOOL — the unstruck bell and seven recurring themes

The sound of Kilmainham will become a composition in its own right. *M* (Fritz Lang, 1931) demonstrates how a recurring sound can acquire narrative identity. *The Third Man* (Carol Reed, 1949) reminds us that a score need not resemble the architecture it accompanies. *The Red Shoes* (Powell and Pressburger, 1948) makes music and movement carry competing commitments. *Fantasia* (1940) provides a vocabulary of abstract motion and musical form; *The Umbrellas of Cherbourg* (Jacques Demy, 1964) gives ordinary transactions the weight of musical expression. *Once Upon a Time in the West* (Sergio Leone, 1968) makes long intervals of environmental sound dramatically consequential.

We will compose seven original motifs, each capable of restraint, grandeur, tenderness, doubt and dissonance. They are not seven moral jingles. A motif should be recognizable through a change of instrument as a person's handwriting remains recognizable through a change of pen. The same motif might arrive as a hummed fragment in the kitchen, a procession of bell tones in the tribunal, or an unresolved interval under the crypt.

The five districts will have their own audible materials: East Wing iron and stone reverberation; West Wing close footsteps and muffled fabric; Archive paper, timber and quiet turning pages; Cloister water, birds and crockery; Undercroft breath, distant dripping and long decay. The historical ages will alter the sonic meaning of the same object. A bell that once announced a shared meal can later summon witnesses.

Directives: write original music rather than imitate film scores; give silence a defined dramatic purpose; distinguish a sound heard by a character from musical accompaniment; never make a crucial fact accessible only by ear. Allow the player to encounter a familiar motif without immediately knowing who or what it belongs to. Musical recognition may invite inquiry but must not substitute for evidence.

Acceptance: one recurring sound with three different historically grounded meanings, one quiet encounter without a score, and a motif heard in contrasting embodiments without moral classification. — ChatGPT-Vitra

## 25 / MINIATURE WORLDS — animation, puppetry and the dignity of KayKit

We are not going to apologize for the stylization of our characters. Classical animation and puppetry teach us that a carefully chosen silhouette can carry an entire personality. Lotte Reiniger's *The Adventures of Prince Achmed* (1926) demonstrates the expressive reach of silhouette and ornament. Jiří Trnka's *The Hand* (1965) turns the relationship between artist and authority into a devastating symbolic encounter. Karel Zeman's *Invention for Destruction* (1958) builds an inhabited world from the texture of an engraving. René Laloux's *Fantastic Planet* (1973) changes the meaning of scale by placing familiar gestures in an unfamiliar civilization. Ray Harryhausen's work in *Jason and the Argonauts* (1963) makes the encounter between mortal bodies and mythological forms physically memorable. The Brothers Quay came later; their antecedents in Central European animation are a useful direction for future study, not a requirement for this classical syllabus.

KayKit's proportions are a strength. We will treat each figure as a carved theatre puppet with a living intention, not a failed photorealistic actor. The ordinary human and the supernatural visitor should be differentiated through bearing, relationships, form and conduct rather than the amount of visual detail lavished upon them. A small figure standing in the great court should feel like a human-sized candle in an immense lantern.

Directives: make a silhouette sheet for each power's preferred and alternate forms; establish distinctive posture and gesture before adding ornament; reserve miniature tableaux for genuinely historical or subjective sequences; make ordinary domestic objects large enough to be legible without turning the whole institution into a toy shop. Let an unfamiliar form perform a familiar gesture. A human inmate must remain recognizable as a particular person even when the mythical figures command the scene's visual attention.

Acceptance: silhouettes can be distinguished without color, three human personalities can be recognized without dialogue, and a demigod's identity can be suggested through a recurring gesture without making its physical appearance proof of its moral condition. — ChatGPT-Vitra

## 26 / THE HISTORICAL EPIC — civilization seen from the kitchen door

We are going to give each of the five ages its own social horizon, rather than five costume parties. *The Fall of the Roman Empire* (Anthony Mann, 1964) shows the grandeur and vulnerability of institutions; *Lawrence of Arabia* (David Lean, 1962) makes landscape dwarf personal ambition, while also inviting scrutiny of its colonial viewpoint. *The Last Command* (Josef von Sternberg, 1928) turns historical upheaval into personal memory. *The Adventures of Robin Hood* (Michael Curtiz and William Keighley, 1938) gives a vivid visual opposition between public authority and alternative fellowship. *The Lion in Winter* (Anthony Harvey, 1968) locates political history in familial rivalry and intimate negotiations. *The Leopard* remains our principal reference for the survival of old forms within new arrangements.

Our monastery will not become a university because a caption says so. A kitchen table becomes a desk for scholarly dispute; its owner can still recall the hungry people who once gathered around it. The Crown will convert an astronomical instrument into a ceremonial object while a minor custodian insists that its earlier purpose matters. The prison will inherit the courtyard, the well and an obligation it does not recognize. Historical transformation is like a tapestry repeatedly turned over: a beautiful pattern on the face, knots and loose threads underneath.

Directives: build every age around a recurring human practice, an inherited structure, an emerging institution and an unresolved obligation. Show history through labor as well as ceremony. Establish two contrasting scales in every epoch: the grand public event and its domestic consequence. Let the demigods encounter unforeseen resistance from ordinary people. Keep all fictional dates and supernatural claims explicitly separate from the actual history of Ireland.

Acceptance: a player can recognize one object, one doorway and one human practice across at least two ages; explain what changed and what endured; and discover a consequence that was never intended by the person who began it. — ChatGPT-Vitra

## 27 / THE ABSURD INSTITUTION — comedy as a form of resistance

The Asylum needs a sense of humor sufficiently humane to survive its darker scenes. Chaplin's *Modern Times* (1936) makes the human body the measure of industrial absurdity; *The Great Dictator* (1940) exposes the theatrical fragility of grandiose authority. Jacques Tati's *Playtime* (1967) demonstrates how architecture itself can become comic without making its inhabitants ridiculous. *The Firemen's Ball* (Miloš Forman, 1967) finds the gap between communal aspiration and the disarray of the actual gathering. *The Government Inspector* tradition, from Gogol through Henry Koster's film *The Inspector General* (1949), offers misplaced institutional certainty. *Kind Hearts and Coronets* (Robert Hamer, 1949) supplies the contrast between elegant manners and alarming ambition.

Our Archive will contain an official who tries to put an unsent letter into the wrong category, a porter who knows where the real records are kept, and a committee that cannot begin until somebody fetches a chair. The joke should be like a tiny loose screw rattling inside an enormous ceremonial clock. We are laughing at the distance between an institution's image and its actual conduct, not at people who are incarcerated or unwell.

Directives: give comedy its own timing; allow a joke to fail; let a human character puncture the pretensions of a supernatural visitor without necessarily defeating its argument. Place two simultaneous ordinary purposes in a shared room: the official is trying to announce something, the caretaker is trying to finish work. Design small rituals that become absurd through repetition, then show someone recovering their original useful purpose. A person can be funny, solemn and mistaken in different circumstances.

Acceptance: a comic ensemble scene that works without the visitor intervening, a humorous misunderstanding with a repair, and an official procedure whose stated purpose is contrasted with its lived consequence. The laughter should make the institution more human, not make suffering less real. — ChatGPT-Vitra

## 28 / THE ACTOR'S BIBLE — the face behind the register

The next character study will look beyond archetypal guards, inmates and supernatural opponents. *The Best Years of Our Lives* (1946), *Brief Encounter* (David Lean, 1945), *Marty* (Delbert Mann, 1955), *Umberto D.* (Vittorio De Sica, 1952), *Bicycle Thieves* (De Sica, 1948), *The Cranes Are Flying* (Mikhail Kalatozov, 1957), and *The 400 Blows* (François Truffaut, 1959) teach us to make the particular person's circumstances more important than the category assigned to them. *Casablanca* (Michael Curtiz, 1942) teaches us that a person's public pose can conceal an unresolved loyalty, and that a decision can matter precisely because it costs something.

For every ordinary human, we will author a private life, an observable habit, a practical skill, a relationship outside the immediate scene, a disagreement they will not easily abandon, and a plausible reason for changing their mind. We will not turn these private matters into automatically available exposition. A warder might be a competent cook; a prisoner might be an impatient music teacher; a clerk might be a devoted gardener. No one is simply a walking vice or gift. The register identifies the role; the actor makes the person.

For the seven powers, we will develop a comparable dramatic vocabulary across their appearances, while preserving their established bipolarities. Each will have a way of entering a room, listening, refusing, making an offer, withdrawing and encountering an unexpected kindness. Their preferred forms are not moral uniforms. An immortal's gesture can recur like an old actor's characteristic pause, recognizable long before their identity is confirmed.

Directives: build scenes of two intentions rather than two moral labels; allow the same gesture to acquire a different meaning through changed context; write the aftermath of a decision, not just its climactic moment. Give vulnerable characters control over what they disclose. Do not turn diagnoses, disability, faith or legal status into shorthand for supernatural corruption.

Acceptance: five ordinary human characters each possess at least one scene unrelated to the seven, one independent desire, one meaningful relationship and one recognizable habit. The dramatic test is whether their absence would be felt even if the main story proceeded unchanged. — ChatGPT-Vitra
